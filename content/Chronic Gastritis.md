# Chronic Gastritis

Two types - Chronic autoimmune and Chronic H pylori

Chronic inflammation of stomach mucosa  

Chronic autoimmune gastritis is due to autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells, which are located in the stomach body and fundus.  

Associated with antibodies against parietal cells and/or intrinsic factor; useful for diagnosis, but pathogenesis is mediated by T cells (type IV hypersensitivity) 

Clinical features
- Atrophy of mucosa with intestinal metaplasia (Fig. 10.11}
- Achlorhydria (low acid production) with increased gastrin levels and antral G-cell hyperplasia
- Megaloblastic (pernicious} anemia due to lack of intrinsic factor iv. Increased risk for gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal type) [[Megaloblastic Anaemia]] most common cause of megaloblastic anaemia

Chronic inflammation induces intestinal metaplasia which increases the risk for gastric adenocarcinoma. Cells in the stomach will turn into cells of the intestinal tract (which are used to being near to inflammatory cells) you will see goblet cells. 

Chronic H pylori gastritis is due to H pylori-induced acute and chronic inflammation; most common form of gastritis (90%) 

H pylori ureases and proteases along with inflammation weaken mucosal defenses; ==antrum is the most common site== (Fig. 10.12).

Presents with epigastric abdominal pain; increased risk for ulceration ([[Peptic Ulcer Disease]]), gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal type), and MALT lymphoma (chronic inflammation leads to germination centres and post germinal centre B cells in the gastric wall)
- This represents the marginal zone 
- The marginal zone only forms when there is a chronic inflammatory state and in this case you have chronic inflammtion. 
- The marginal zone can result in MALT lymphoma. [[Gastric MALT Lymphoma]]

Treatment involves triple therapy.
Resolves gastritis/ulcer and reverses intestinal metaplasia
Negative urea breath test and lack of stool antigen confirm eradication of H pylori.
