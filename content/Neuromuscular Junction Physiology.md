# Neuromuscular Junction Physiology — End-Plate Potentials & Iontophoresis

ACh is released from the presynaptic terminal in discrete quanta (vesicles). A single vesicle released spontaneously at rest produces a miniature end-plate potential (MEPP). When an action potential arrives, calcium influx synchronises the release of many vesicles at once, summing into a full end-plate potential (EPP) — unlike a CNS EPSP, a single EPP is normally large enough on its own to trigger a muscle action potential, giving the NMJ a large safety factor.

Iontophoresis is the technique of ejecting a charged neurotransmitter (such as ACh) from a micropipette using a small electric current, applied to a precise patch of postsynaptic membrane, used experimentally to map ACh receptor density. In normal muscle, receptors are dense only directly under the nerve terminal, so iontophoretic ACh only produces a depolarisation there. After denervation, receptors spread across the entire muscle fibre (denervation supersensitivity), so iontophoresis anywhere on the fibre now produces a response.

Clinical contrast: [[Lambert-Eaton Syndrome]] involves antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing ACh release and EPP amplitude (improves with repetitive stimulation) — as opposed to myasthenia gravis, where antibodies target postsynaptic ACh receptors directly, also reducing EPP amplitude but without the repetitive-stimulation improvement.
