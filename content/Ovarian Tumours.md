# Ovarian Tumours

1. Ovary is composed of three cell types: surface epithelium, germ cells, and sex cord-stroma.
    
2. Tumor can arise from any of these cell types or from metastases.

Benign ovarian cysts are extremely common. They may be divided into physiological cysts, benign germ cell tumours, benign epithelial tumours and benign sex cord stromal tumours.  
  
Complex (i.e. multi-loculated) ovarian cysts should be biopsied to exclude malignancy.  

##### Physiological cysts (functional cysts)

Follicular cysts  

- commonest type of ovarian cyst
- due to non-rupture of the dominant follicle or failure of atresia in a non-dominant follicle
- commonly regress after several menstrual cycles

Corpus luteum cyst  

- during the menstrual cycle if pregnancy doesn't occur the corpus luteum usually breaks down and disappears. If this doesn't occur the corpus luteum may fill with blood or fluid and form a corpus luteal cyst
- more likely to present with intraperitoneal bleeding than follicular cysts

##### Benign germ cell tumours

Dermoid cyst  

- also called mature cystic teratomas. Usually lined with epithelial tissue and hence may contain skin appendages, hair and teeth
- most common benign ovarian tumour in woman under the age of 30 years
- median age of diagnosis is 30 years old
- bilateral in 10-20%
- usually asymptomatic. Torsion is more likely than with other ovarian tumours

##### Benign epithelial tumours

Arise from the ovarian surface epithelium  
  
Serous cystadenoma  

- the most common benign epithelial tumour which bears a resemblance to the most common type of ovarian cancer (serous carcinoma)
- bilateral in around 20%

Mucinous cystadenoma  

- second most common benign epithelial tumour
- they are typically large and may become massive
- if ruptures may cause pseudomyxoma peritonei

#####  Surface derived tumours

These account for around 65% of ovarian tumours, including the greatest number of malignant tumours.  

Risk of epithelial ovarian tumours tracks with lifetime number of ovulatory cycles ("incessant ovulation" theory) — each ovulation causes a small wound in the surface epithelium that needs repairing, and repeated repair cycles raise the chance of a mutation. Nulliparity is therefore a risk factor; parity and continuous COCP use are both protective, since pregnancy and the COCP both suppress ovulation.

|Tumour|Benign/malignant|Notes|
|---|---|---|
|Serous cystadenoma|Benign|Most common benign ovarian tumour, often bilateral  <br>Cyst lined by ciliated cells (similar to Fallopian tube)|
|Serous cystadenocarcinoma|Malignant|Often bilateral  <br>Psammoma bodies seen (collection of calcium)|
|Mucinous cystadenoma|Benign|Cyst lined by mucous-secreting epithelium (similar to endocervix)|
|Mucinous cystadenocarcinoma|Malignant|May be associated with pseudomyxoma peritonei (although mucinous tumour of appendix is the more common cause)|
|Brenner tumour|Benign|Contain Walthard cell rests (benign cluster of epithelial cells), similar to transitional cell epithelium. Typically have 'coffee bean' nuclei.|

##### Germ cell tumours

These are more common in adolescent girls and are account for 15-20% of tumours. Similar cancer types to those seen in the testicle.  

|Tumour|Benign/malignant|Notes|
|---|---|---|
|Teratoma|Mature teratoma (dermoid cyst) - most common: benign  <br>Immature teratoma: malignant|Account for 90% of germ cell tumours  <br>Contain a combination of ectodermal (e.g. hair), mesodermal (e.g. bone) and endodermal tissue|
|Dysgerminoma|Malignant|Most common malignant germ cell tumour  <br>Histological appearance similar to that of testicular seminoma  <br>Associated with Turner's syndrome  <br>Typically secrete hCG and LDH|
|Yolk sac tumour|Malignant|Typically secrete AFP  <br>Schiller-Duval bodies on histology are pathognomonic|
|Choriocarcinoma|Malignant|Rare tumour that is part of the spectrum gestational trophoblastic disease  <br>Typically have increased hCG levels  <br>Often characterised by early haematogenous spread to the lungs|

##### Sex cord-stromal tumours

Represent around 3-5% of ovarian tumours. Often produce hormones.  

|Tumour|Benign/malignant|Notes|
|---|---|---|
|Granulosa cell tumour|Malignant|Produces oestrogen leading to precocious puberty if in children or endometrial hyperplasia in adults.  <br>Marker: inhibin (also normally secreted by granulosa cells as part of the FSH feedback loop)  <br>Contains Call-Exner bodies (small eosinophilic fluid-filled spaces between granulosa cells)|
|Sertoli-Leydig cell tumour|Benign|Produces androgens → masculinizing effects  <br>Associated with Peutz-Jegher syndrome|
|Fibroma|Benign|Associated with Meigs' syndrome (ascites, pleural effusion)  <br>Solid tumour consisting of bundles of spindle-shaped fibroblasts  <br>Typically occur around the menopause, classically causing a pulling sensation in the pelvis|

##### Metastatic tumours

Account for around 5% of tumours.  

| Tumour            | Benign/malignant | Notes                                                                                                    |
| ----------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Krukenberg tumour | Malignant        | Metastases from a gastrointestinal tumour resulting in a mucin-secreting signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma, classically bilateral. Classic teaching describes transcoelomic (peritoneal) seeding as the spread mechanism, but current evidence favours lymphatic spread as the primary route for a gastric primary (gastric and ovarian lymphatics are anatomically close), with a real haematogenous contribution too — transcoelomic spread appears less common than traditionally taught. |
