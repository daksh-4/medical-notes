# Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

Fibrosis of the lung interstitium. Wall of the alveolar sac is very thick by fibrosis. 

Aetiology is related to cyclical lung injury with unknown cause
- TGF-beta from injured pneumocytes induces fibrosis for the healing response. 
- Repetitive lung microinjuries result in the loss of type 1 pneumocytes, which compose 95% of the gas exchange surface. 
- Type 1 are incapable of replication, normal repair requires the proliferation of type 2 with subsequent differentiation into type 1 
- Type 2 undergo reactive hyperplasia but fail to differentiate because of dysfunctional cell fate pathways (Wnt/transforming growth factor beta) and abnormalities of the underlying basement membrane. 
- Impaired re-epithelialisation causes lung fibroblasts to undergo focal proliferation and begin secreting excessive amounts of collagen, leading to interstitial fibrosis
- Fibrosis exerts radial traction on the distal airways, leading to dilation of the terminal bronchioles and producing the honeycomb appearance.


Secondary causes such as drugs (bleomycin and amiodarone) and radiation therapy must be excluded.

Clinical features
- Progressive dyspnoea and cough due to damage 
- Fibrosis on lung CT (fibrosis begins in the subpleural regions) 
- Treatment is lung transplantation

Pneumoconioses
- Interstitial fibrosis due to occupational exposure
- Requires chronic exposure to small particles that are fibrogenic. Alveolar macrophage causing fibrosis to wall off the small particles. 
- Coal worker's pneumoconiosis - carbon dust black lung that is shrunken
	- associated with RA (Caplan syndrome), 
	- mild exposure results in anthracosis (collections of carbon-laden macrophages) but not significant
- Silicosis - seen in sandblasters and silica miners
	- Fibrotic nodules in the upper lobes of the lung 
	- Increased risk for TB silica impairs phagolysosome formation by macrophages (only one) 
- Berylliosis - beryllium miners and workers in the aerospace industry
	- Non caseating granulomas in the lung, hilar lymph nodes and systemic organs (looks very similar to sarcoidosis)
	- Increased risk for lung cancer
- Asbestosis - seen in construction workers, plumbers and shipyard workers
	- Fibrosis of lung and pleura with increased risk for lung carcinoma and mesothelioma. Lung carcinoma is more common than mesothelioma in exposed individuals. 
	- Lesions may contain long, golden-brown fibers with associated iron
