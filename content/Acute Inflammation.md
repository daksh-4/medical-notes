# Acute Inflammation

- If neutrophils are the primary cells coming out into the tissue space then it is acute inflammation, if lymphocytes then it is chronic
    
- Acute inflammation is characterised by oedema and neutrophils in tissue
    
- In response to infection or tissue necrosis with the goal of eliminating the pathogen or clearing pathogenic debris.
    
- Innate Immune System Mediators
    
    - Toll like Receptors presents on cells of the innate immune system such as neutrophils and dendritic cells. CD14 on macrophages recognises lipopolysaccharide on the outer membrane of gram negative bacteria. TLR activation results in up regulation of NF-kappaB which activates immune response genes which leads to the production of multiple immune mediators.
    - Arachidonic acid - released from the phospholipid cell membrane by phospholipase A2 which is then acted on by cyclooxygenase or 5-lipooxygenase
        - Cyclooxygenase produces prostaglandins - PGI2, PGD2, PGE2 mediating vasodilation and increased vascular permeability. PGE2 also mediates fever and pain
        - 5-lipooxygenase produces leukotriene. LTB4 attracts and activates neutrophils. LTC4, LTD4, LTE4 mediate vasoconstriction, bronchospasm and increased vascular permeability from contraction of pericytes. There are 4 things that activate neutrophils - LTB4, C5a, IL8 and bacterial products.

    - Mast cells - activated by tissue trauma, C3a and C5a, cross linking of cell surface IgE by antigen. Responds by releasing histamine granules which mediate vasodilation and increasing vascular permeability in the post capillary venule. Delayed response producing arachidonic acid metabolites particularly leukotrienes which maintain the acute inflammatory response.

    - Complement - circulate as inactive precursors
        - Classical pathway - C1 binds to IgG or IgM that is bound to antigen. GM makes classic cars
        - Alternative pathway - microbial products directly activate complement
        - Mannose-binding lectin pathway - MBL binds mannose on microorganisms and activates complement
        - These pathways eventually lead to the production of C3 convertase (making C3a and C3b) which eventually makes C5 convertase (making C5a and C5b) then eventually C5b joining with C6, C7, C8, C9 to form the membrane attack complex.
        - C3a and C5a trigger mast cell degranulation. C5a is chemotactic for neutrophils, C3b is opsonin for phagocytosis, MAC lyses microbes by creating holes in the cell membrane
    - Hageman factor - inactive pro inflammatory protein produced in the liver activated upon exposure to sub endothelial or tissue collagen. Plays an important role in DIC. In gram negative sepsis, the bacteria are able to activate Hageman factor. Hageman activates coagulation and fibrinolytic systems, complement and kinin systems.
        - High molecular weight kininogen is cleaved to bradykinin which mediates vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and pain (like PGE2). They mediate pain by sensitizing sensory nerve endings.
- Fever - pyrogens (such as LPS from GN bacteria) cause macrophages to release IL-1 and TNF, increasing COX activity in the perivascular cells of the hypothalamus, increased PGE2 raises temperatures set point

The goal of the initial phase is to bring fluid and oedema, the next phase is the neutrophil phase (peaking around 24 hours), the third phase is the macrophage phase which peaks around 2-3 days.

- Vaosidilation occurs and slows the blood flow in the post capillary venules, allowing cells to marginate from the centre of the flow to the periphery
- Rolling - Cells that have marinated slow down via selecting which are unregulated on the endothelial interior surface.
    - P-selectin is released from Weibel Palade bodies (also make von Willebrand’s factor) mediated by histamine and E selectin is induced by TNF and IL-1. Selectins bind sialyl Lewis X on leukocytes which results in rolling of the leukocytes along vessel wall. (all white blood cells use this mechanism)
- Adhesion - Cellular adhesion molecules are upregulated on the endothelium by TNF and IL-1. Integrins unregulated on leukocytes by C5a and LTB4. The interaction between these molecules results in firm adhesion to the vessel wall.
    - Autosomal recessive defect of integrins (CD18 subunit) leads to leukocyte adhesion deficiency. Clinical features - delayed separation of the umbilical cord (blood supply to umbilical cord is cut off, necrosis happens and acute inflammation separates the umbilical cord but in this condition it doesn’t happen), increased circulating neutrophils, recurrent bacterial infections that lack pus formation.
- Transmigration and chemotaxis - leukocytes transmigrate across the endothelium of post capillary venules
    - Move towards chemical attractants - IL-8, C5a, LTB4 and bacterial products
- Phagocytosis - consumption of pathogens or necrotic tissue. Enhanced by opsonins like IgG and C3b. Neutrophils recognise these molecules and eat whatever it is bound to.
    - Pseudopods extend to form phagosomes, internalising the pathogen and merging with lysosomes to form phagolysosomes.
    - Chediak Higashi Syndrome - protein trafficking defect (autosomal recessive), characterised by impaired phagolysosome formation. Moving a phagosome to the lysosome requires transport by microtubules which are defective in this syndrome. Clinical features - increased risk of pyogenic infections, neutropenia (microtubules can’t pull cells apart in mitosis), giant granules in leukocytes, defective primary haemostasis (these two happen because granules in cells can’t be evenly distributed), albinism (melanocyte produces pigment then passes it along to keratinocyte), peripheral neuropathy ( you need microtubules to keep the peripheral nerves alive because they are 2ft long).
- Destruction of phagocytose material - Oxygen dependent
    - O2 dependent killing- HOCl generated by oxidative burst in phagolysosomes destroys phagocytosed microbes. Poor in chronic granulomatous disease due to NADPH oxidase (X-linked or autosomal recessive). Patients get infection with catalase positive organisms (S Aureus, Pseudomonas cepacia, Serratia marcescens, Nocardia and Aspergillus). Most bacteria naturally produce hydrogen peroxide so you could convert it to bleach and kill them so most are not a problem. Some bacteria produce catalase which breaks down hydrogen peroxide meaning bleach can no longer be produced. Nitroblue tetrazolium test and turns blue if NADPH oxidase can convert O2 to O2- remains colourless if not.
    - MPO deficiency - NBT test is normal, most patients are asymptomatic and increased risk for candida infections
        - O2 converted to O2- by NADPH oxidase
        - O2- converted to H2O2 by superoxide dismutase
        - H2O2 converted to HOCl radical (bleach) by myeloperoxidase
- Oxygen independent killing
    - Less effective and occurs via enzymes present in the leukocyte secondary granules like lysozyme and major basic protein
- Resolution - neutrophils undergo apoptosis and disappear within 24 hours after resolution of inflammatory stimulus. Leave pus behind which is dead neutrophils.
- Macrophages predominate after neutrophils and peak 2-3 days after inflammation begins. Derived from monocytes in the blood (which are called macrophages in the tissue). Enter the tissue the same way as the neutrophils. They destroy phagocytoses materia using the lysozyme.
- Macrophages manage the next step of the acute inflammatory process
    - Resolution and healing via IL-10 and TGF ß
    - Continued acute inflammation via IL-8 calling in more neutrophils
    - Abscess
    - Chronic inflammation
