# Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome

Respiratory distress due to inadequate surfactant levels because of messed up type 2 pneumocytes

Surfactant reduces surface tension in the lung to prevent the alveoli from collapsing. 

Clinical features
- Increasing respiratory effort after birth because they are struggling to open the lungs. 
- Tachypnoea with use of accessory muscles and grunting
- Hypoxaemia with cyanosis
- Diffuse granularity of the lung on X ray
- ![[Pasted image 20240926223133.png|500]]

Associated with 
- [[Prematurity]] screen with L:S (lecithin to sphingomyelin) ratio
	- Adequate levels of surfactant are only reached at 34 weeks
	- as the lung makes surfactant, lecithin increases and sphingomyelin stays the same. Once the ratio is bigger than 2 then there is enough. 
	- Major component is phosphatidylcholine (which is the same as lecithin)
- C section delivery 
	- Steroids increase the production of surfactant and cause release of surfactant from the pneumocytes
	- No stress during birth so no surfactant released
- Maternal diabetes
	- High blood sugar means the baby's pancreas will make more insulin which inhibits surfactant production

Complications
- Hypoxaemia increases risk for persistence of patent ductus arteriosus (because PDA only causes when there is adequate oxygenation of the blood) and necrotizing enterocolitis (because of decreased oxygen going to the gut)
- Supplemental oxygen increases risk for free radical injury
	- Can injure the retina and cause blindness
	- If an immature lung is given a lot of oxygen, there is a lot of free radicals produced which can lead to bronchopulmonary dysplasia
