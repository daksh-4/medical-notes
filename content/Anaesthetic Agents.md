# Anaesthetic Agents

### Inhaled Anaesthetics

Solubility of the gas for blood determines onset/offset 

Solubility of the gas for lipids determines the potency

![[Pasted image 20241105094918.png|500]]

Molecules dissolved in blood do not give any anaesthetic effect, only molecules not dissolved. So you need to saturate the blood to generate a partial pressure. The more soluble it is in blood, the longer it takes to kick in. 

To determine which drugs are more soluble in blood, use blood/gas partition coefficient
- [blood] / [alveoli]

Lipid solubility
- Affinity of gas for lipids
- Oil/gas partition coefficient
- increased lipid affinity = more potent
- However this is not really used in practice

Use minimum alveolar concentration instead
- Concentration of anaesthetic that prevents movement in 50% of subjects in response to pain
- Low MAC = high potency
- MAC changes with age
- Lipid solubility = 1/MAC
- When using multiple drugs, MACs are additive allows use of lower concentration of each drug, leading to lower risk of adverse effects

Inhaled anaesthetics
- Isoflurane, sevoflurane (main ones)
- Desflurane, halothane, enflurane, methoxyflurane, nitrous oxide
- All cause myocardial and respiratory depression
- Nausea and vomiting
- Cause cerebral vasodilation 
- Decreased GFT
- Halothane - hepatoxicity and malignant hyperthermia dangerous
- Methoxyflurane - nephrotoxicity 
- Enflurane - lowers the seizure threshold

### Intravenous Anaesthetics

Barbituates, benzos, opioids, etomidate, ketamine, propofol

Barbituates
- Binding to GABA
- High potency from high lipid solubility
- Rapid onset
- Ultra short acting due to rapid distribution to muscle and fat
- Myocardial and respiratory depression
- Decreased cerebral blood flow

Benzodiazepines
- Bind to GABA
- Increase frequency of GABA ion channel opening 
- Low dose - anti anxiety 
- High dose - sedation, amnesia and anticonvulsant
- Cause cardio-respiratory depression 
- Overdose - flumazenil
- Midazolam - short procedures like endoscopy

Opioids 
- Sedatives and analgesics
- No amnesia 
- Act on opioid (mu) receptors in the brain
	- G protein linked
	- Increases K efflux from cells
	- This hyperpolarises the cell so it's harder to conduct an impulse, decreasing pain
- Side effects
	- Down respiratory drive
	- Down BP
	- Nausea and vomiting
	- Ileus 
	- Urinary retention 
- Tolerance after chronic use
	- Less effect on pain and a lot of side effects
	- But no tolerance to constipation or [[Miosis]], these effects persist 
- Naloxone
	- Mu antagonist
	- Competes with opioids and displaces them from binding sites
	- Reverses effects within minutes
	- Must be given IV/nasal because it gets inactivated by the liver

Ketamine
- PCP derivative
- Antagonist of NMDA receptor (glutamate)
- Dissociative drug
	- Trance 
	- analgesia and amnesia
	- few resp or cardio side effects
- If used alone, they get emergence reactions
	- Disorientations 
	- Dreams, hallucinations
	- Co administer midazolam

Etomidate
- ﻿﻿Modulates GABA receptors
- ﻿﻿Blocks neuroexcitation
- ﻿﻿Anesthesia but not analgesia
- Relatively hemodynamically neutral
	- Good for hypotensive patients
- ﻿﻿Blocks cortisol synthesis
- ﻿﻿Rapid sequence intubation

Propofol
- GABA modulator
- Sedation and amnesia
- Myocardial depression and hypotension

Induction
- Propofol, etomidate or ketamine

Paralysis
- Rocuronium

Maintenance
- Propofol, fentanyl, sevoflurane or desflurane
