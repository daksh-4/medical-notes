[[Nephrology]]

In the countercurrent exchange there are four processes occurring simultaneously:

1. The thick ascending limb pumps salt out.
2. The descending limb loses water.
3. Tubular fluid keeps flowing, bringing in new fluid.
4. The vasa recta preserves the gradient.

The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps sodium chloride out by the NKCC2 transporter and the sodium-potassium ATPase and is also impermeable to water. 

The descending limb responds. It has zero NaCl transport and enormous water permeability so water is leaving and sodium chloride is staying. 

New isotonic fluid arrives from the proximal tubule. Water gets pumped out in the descending limb and then sodium gets pumped out in the thick ascending limb. So one limb is concentrating, the other is diluting, and the flow is in the opposite direction. 

The goal of this is to increase the amount of sodium fluoride in the interstitial space, making the tissue around the tubule more concentrated.

this matters for the collecting duct because if ADH is present, water moves from low osmolality in the tubule to high osmolality in the interstitium and water leaves the tubule because the interstitium is salty. That is the entire point. If the interstitium was not concentrated there would be no osmotic gradient. That would mean that the collecting duct could not reabsorb water even with ADH, urine is dilute, and there is no ability to conserve water. clinically this is what happens with loop diuretics, which collapse the medullary gradient by affecting the NKCC2 receptor
