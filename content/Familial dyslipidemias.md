[[Endocrinology]] [[Apolipoprotein]]

Type 1 can't break down chylomicrons due to a deficiency of LPL or APOC2, which activates LPL. Chylomicrons are full of triglycerides, and if LPL can't hydrolyze the triglyceride, the chylomicrons remain in the blood. The result is high chylomicrons and high triglycerides, but no increased risk of atherosclerosis because the chylomicrons are enormous and cannot enter the arterial wall. Clinically, this looks like childhood onset with recurrent pancreatitis, eruptive xanthomas, milky plasma, and hepatosplenomegaly. 

Type 2A: the defect is in the LDL receptor and sometimes in APOB100. Normally, LDL binds to the LDL receptor and enters the liver. If the receptor doesn't work, LDL can't be cleared, which increases LDL only, but the triglycerides stay normal. Very high LDL results in premature coronary artery disease. 

Type 2b: the liver is making too much VLDL. VLDL eventually becomes LDL, so increased VLDL means there is more LDL later. Therefore, increased cholesterol and increased triglycerides are also associated with premature cardiovascular disease. 

Type 3: The key protein is APOE, which allows the liver to recognise remnant. Chylomicron becomes a chylomicron remnant and needs APOE to bind to the liver. Likewise, VLDL becomes IDL, which also needs APOE to bind to the liver. Without APOE, the remnants accumulate, so this is not LDL based. It is chylomicron remnants and IDL, which results in increased cholesterol and triglycerides, also leading to a risk of premature atherosclerosis. 

Type 4 is familial hypertriglyceridemia. The liver also overproduces VLDL, which mainly carries triglycerides. No xanthomas usually. 

Type 5 is a combination of type 1 and 4, with massively elevated triglycerides due to increased colour microns in LDL.
