# Sideroblastic Anaemia

Sideroblastic anaemia is due to defective protoporphyrin synthesis 

Decreased protoporphyrin means less haeme is produced which leads to microcytic anaemia. 

Protoporphyrin is synthesised via a series of reactions
- Final reaction attaches protoporphyrin to iron to make hame this occurs in the mitochondria of the erythroblast

1. Succinyl coA is converted to amino lavillunic acid by amino lavillunic acid synthase (rate limiting step). Vitamin B6 is a cofactor for this enzyme
2. ALA is converted to porpho bilinogen by ALA dehydrogenase
3. Porpho biilnogen undergoes a series of reactions to make protoporphyrin
4. Protoporphyrin is combined with iron to make haeme by ferro chelatase

Whilst these reactions are occurring, iron is transferred to erythroid precursor. Iron enters the erythroblast mitochondria from the bone marrow macrophage to form haeme. If protoporphyrin is deficient, iron remains trapped in the mitochondria of the erythroblast. This creates a ring of iron filled mitochondria around the nucleus forming classic "RINGED SIDEROBLASTS". Prussian blue stain marks iron.

Causes
- Congenital defect most commonly involves ALAS (amino lavillunic acid synthase) which is the rate limiting enzyme in the production of protoporphyrin. 
- Acquired causes
	- Alcoholism (mitochondrial poison)
	- Lead poisoning (denatures ALAD and ferro chelatase)
	- Vitamin B6 used as a cofactor for ALAS in the rate limiting step in the production of protoporphyrin. The common circumstance is isoniazid therapy. 

Lab findings
- Iron overloaded state
- Increased ferritin and decreased TIBC
- Increased serum iron and increased percentage saturation. 
Just like iron studies in haemochromatosis
