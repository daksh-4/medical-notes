# Gastric Carcinoma

Malignant proliferation of surface epithelial cells (adenocarcinoma) 

Subclassified into intestinal and diffuse types

Intestinal type (more common) 
- presents as a large, irregular ulcer with heaped up margins; most commonly involves the lesser curvature of the antrum (similar to gastric ulcer)
- Risk factors include intestinal metaplasia (e.g., due to chronic H pylori and chronic autoimmune gastritis), nitrosamines in smoked foods (Japan), and blood type A.  

Diffuse type 
- is characterized by signet ring cells that diffusely infiltrate the gastric wall (Fig. l0.14B); 
	- Signet ring cell is a cell that has the nucleus that is pushed to the edge by mucin in the cell
- desmoplasia results in thickening of stomach wall (leads to linitis plastica, Fig. 10.14A). 
	- Desmoplasia is a reactive response of the stroma to the invasive tumour
- Not associated with H pylori, intestinal metaplasia, or nitrosamines

Gastric carcinoma presents late with weight loss, abdominal pain, anemia, and early satiety (due to fibrosis of the stomach preventing it from expanding)

Rarely presents as acanthosis nigricans or Leser-Trelat sign (dozens of seborrhaeic keratoses which arise all of a sudden)

Spread to lymph nodes can involve the left supraclavicular node (Virchow node).

Distant metastasis most commonly involves liver; other sites include

Periumbilical region (Sister Mary Joseph nodule); seen with intestinal type

Bilateral ovaries (Krukenberg tumor); seen with diffuse type
