# Myocardial Infarction

Necrosis of cardiac myocytes

Due to rupture of atherosclerotic plaque with thrombosis and complete occlusion of coronary artery

Other causes include coronary artery vasospasm, emboli and [[Vasculitis]] (e.g. Kawasaki disease)

Severe crushing chest pain lasting longer than 20 minutes
Sweating
Dyspnoea
Symptoms not relieved by nitrates

Usually involves the left ventricle and the RV and atria are spared. Most common artery involved is left anterior descending. So you get necrosis of the anterior ventricular wall and the anterior interventricular septum 

Occlusion of the right coronary artery you get infarction of the posterior ventricular wall and the posterior interventricular septum. 

If the left circumflex is involved, there is infarction of the left ventricular wall. 

Initial Phase
- Subendocardial infarction involving less than 50% of myocardial thickness
- ECG shows ST segment depression

Lab tests detect elevated cardiac enzymes
- Membrane damage is the sign of irreversible damage
- Troponin I
	- Rises 2-4 hours after infarction
	- Peaks at 24 hours 
	- Returns to normal by 7-10 days
- CK-MB
	- Rises 4-6 hours 
	- Peaks at 24
	- Returns to normal by 72
	- Used to detect reinfarction

Aspirin/heparin, oxygen, nitrates, beta blocker, ace inhibitor

Fibrinolysis or angioplasty
- Results in return of blood flow to an area of dead heart
- Contraction band necrosis - calcium enters into dead cells causing contraction

- Reperfusion injury
	- Free radicals can further injure the myocardium
	- Cardiac enzymes will continue to rise

coagulative necrosis

Inflammation beginning with neutrophils and progressing to macrophages

1 week

Granulation tissue

1 month

Scar (with type I collagen)

You get [[Pericarditis]] because the inflammation in the transmural infarction leaks into the pericardium. (only occurs in transmural)

Macrophages increase risk of rupture (ventricular free wall which leads to tamponade), septum or papillary muscle necrosis. 

Papillary muscle is fed by the right coronary artery. 

The scar is not as strong as the cardiac muscle, increasing the risk of aneurysm (leading to mural thrombus). 

Dressler syndrome - inflammation in the pericardium could expose the pericardial antigens to the immune system. This leads to an autoimmune [[Pericarditis]].
