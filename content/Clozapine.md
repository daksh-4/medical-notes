# Clozapine: Mechanism, Monitoring and Serious Adverse Effects

Clozapine is reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failure of two adequate antipsychotic trials). Unlike typical/most atypical antipsychotics, it binds D2 receptors more weakly and dissociates faster ("fast-off D2" theory), while also engaging D1, D3, D4, 5-HT2A, 5-HT2C, muscarinic, H1, and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors. This broad, comparatively loose receptor profile is thought to underlie its efficacy where tighter D2-selective drugs fail, though the precise mechanism behind its superior efficacy in treatment resistance is not fully established.

Agranulocytosis — idiosyncratic, immune-mediated reaction; not dose-dependent or cumulative. Risk is highest early (particularly weeks 6–18) and falls substantially after the first year of stable treatment, which is why monitoring frequency tapers over time rather than staying constant.

Older/traditional ANC+WBC monitoring schedule (still widely taught): weekly for 18 weeks, then fortnightly until 1 year, then monthly indefinitely.

Revised schedule (MHRA/EMA, September 2025 — current standard, supersedes the above): weekly for 18 weeks → monthly for the remaining 34 weeks (completing year 1) → every 12 weeks from year 2 (if no neutropenia history) → annually after 2 years clear. WBC monitoring has been dropped; ANC alone is tracked. Initiation requires ANC ≥1.5×10⁹/L (≥1.0×10⁹/L for benign ethnic neutropenia). Treatment is stopped at ANC <1.0×10⁹/L (<0.5×10⁹/L for benign ethnic neutropenia).

Myocarditis — onset clusters early, typically days 14–22 of treatment. Baseline echo, troponin, and CRP before starting; troponin and CRP weekly for the first 4 weeks alongside routine FBC; resting heart rate ≥120 bpm prompts closer surveillance. Continue with daily biomarker monitoring if troponin <2× ULN or CRP 50–100 mg/L. Stop clozapine immediately and refer to cardiology if troponin ≥2× ULN or CRP >100 mg/L.

Other notable adverse effects: dose-related seizure risk, marked metabolic syndrome/weight gain, hypersalivation (paradoxical M4 receptor agonism despite antimuscarinic activity elsewhere), and GI hypomotility/ileus — unlike agranulocytosis, this risk does build with cumulative anticholinergic exposure and is monitored through bowel-habit vigilance rather than blood tests.
