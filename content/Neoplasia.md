# Neoplasia

Defined as new tissue growth that is unregulated, irreversible and monoclonal. It is distinct from hyperplasia and repair. 

Monoclonal - neoplastic cells are derived from a single mother cell. Clonality can be determined by G6PD isoforms or androgen receptor isoforms. 
- There are 4 isoforms and you inherit one from your mother and one from your father. 
- In females, one of the X chromosome is randomly inactivated so only one of the isoforms is active in each cell. If you look at all the cells, the ratio of the two isoforms is 1:1. 
- If a women gets hyperplasia of her uterine wall, cells divide and the ratio remains 1:1 because the cells are from a polyclonal population
- If only one cell is dividing for example in a tumour. Then all the daughter cells will have the same isoform. So ratio is different. 

Clonality of B cells is determined by Ig light chain phenotype.
- B cells in the lymph nodes either express kappa or beta light chain
- The ratio is normally 3:1 (kappa:beta)
- In an infection, there is hyperplasia of the cells in the lymph node and the ratio remains the same. 
- If there is a neoplastic proliferation, one cell is going to be making all of the malignant cells. This changes the kappa:lamda ratio. This can either be metastatic or a lymphoma. 

Neoplastic tumours are benign or malignant
- Benign tumours remain localised and do not metastasise 
- Malignant tumours invade locally and have the potential to metastasise.

Approximately 30 divisions occur before the earliest symptoms arise.
- Each division results in increased mutations
- Cancers that do not produce symptoms until late in disease will have undergone additional divisions and mutations
- Cancers that are detected late tend to have a poor prognosis.
