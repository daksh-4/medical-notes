![[Pasted image 20240901174234.png]]

Mobile means that the mass is not invasive and attaching itself to the basement membrane.

Benign tumours look similar to the tissue they are growing in. 

Malignant tumours are usually poorly differentiated and doesn't look like the tissue. The nuclei will be differently sized and dark blue. The nucleus is normally large.

Left is benign and right is malignant. You can see pink colloid (homogenous non-crystalline substance consisting of large molecules or ultramicroscopic particles of one substance dispersed through a second substance) in the middle of the tumour cells on the left image. 

### Immunohistochemistry 
Used to characterise malignant tumours that are difficult to classify on histology. For example cells that are very poorly differentiated so you can't tell if it's a sarcoma, melanoma, lymphoma etc. 
- Dye some antibodies and let them bind to the cancer cells. 
- This tells you what proteins are present in the cells. 

![[Pasted image 20240903162236.png]]

Intermediate filaments vary depending on the type of cell you are dealing with. 

### Serum tumour markers

Useful as a screening test, monitoring response to treatment and monitoring recurrence. Elevated levels require tissue biopsy for diagnosis of carcinoma. 

### Grading of cancer

Does the tumour resemble the parent tissue that it comes from? 
Done by pathologist who takes into account the architectural and nuclear features

Well differentiated: resembles parent tissue. 

Well differentiated cancers have better prognosis than poorly differentiated cancers

### Staging of Cancer

Size and spread
Key prognostic factor and is only properly determined after final resection of the tumour. 

TNM staging

T - Tumour size or depth of invasion. Size is important in solid tumours (e.g breast) and depth is important in tubular cancers (e.g bowel).

N - spread to regional lymph nodes

M - metastasis; single most important prognostic factor.
