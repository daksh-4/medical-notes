# Syphilis

## Serological Testing

Two categories of test are used together, never alone:

Non-treponemal tests (RPR, VDRL) — detect antibodies against cardiolipin, a phospholipid released from host cells damaged during infection, not from the organism itself. Nonspecific: false positives occur with pregnancy, SLE/antiphospholipid syndrome, other infections, and IV drug use. Titre correlates with disease activity, so these are used to monitor treatment response — a fourfold drop in titre is the marker of adequate treatment. Some patients remain "serofast," with a persistent low positive titre despite cure, so a fully negative result isn't guaranteed even after successful treatment.

Treponemal-specific tests (TPPA, TPHA, EIA/CLIA) — detect antibodies against T. pallidum itself. More specific, used to confirm the diagnosis. Typically remain positive for life regardless of treatment, so they're not useful for monitoring response.

Diagnosis requires a positive non-treponemal test confirmed by a positive treponemal-specific test — neither is diagnostic alone.

## Neurosyphilis / CSF Testing

Lumbar puncture/CSF examination is only indicated with clinical evidence of neurological involvement, not as routine screening. A negative serum non-treponemal test is close to 100% sensitive for excluding CSF abnormality, so a negative serum RPR/VDRL alone can reasonably exclude neurosyphilis.

- CSF VDRL (or CSF RPR) — specific but poorly sensitive; misses a substantial proportion of true cases (under 50% sensitive for ocular syphilis, under 10% for otosyphilis).
- CSF TPHA (TPPA has been withdrawn in the UK) — a titre >1:320 is sensitive and specific for neurosyphilis; lower positive titres are harder to interpret, since antibodies can passively leak into CSF from serum across a damaged blood-brain barrier without true intrathecal infection.

Results are interpreted alongside the clinical picture, not any single test in isolation.
