# Thalassaemia

Where is alpha thalassaemia gene mutation located?

What are the different gene deletions you can get?

Why is 4 gene deletion in alpha lethal?

Decreased synthesis of globin chains leading to reduced haemoglobin production which results in a microcytic anaemia. 

Inherited mutation (carriers are protected against Plasmodium falciparum malaria)

Divided in to alpha and beta based on the decreased production of alpha or beta globin chains. Normal types of haemoglobin are HbF (2 alphas 2 gamma), HbA (2 alphas and 2 betas) and HBA2 (2 alphas 2 deltas)

### Alpha Thalassaemia 

Usually due to gene deletion. Normally 4 alpha alleles are present on chromosome 16 ie 2 separate alpha globulin genes located on each chromosome 16. 

If 1 gene deleted, the patients are asymptomatic 

If 2 genes are deleted then patients get a mild anaemia with a slightly increased RBC count. There are two possibilites: if you knock out both genes on one chromosome (cis) or one on both chromosomes (trans). Cis is worse because it is associated with increased risk of severe thalassaemia in offspring and is more commonly seen in Asians. 

People say that this is the reason for higher rate of spontaneous abortions in Asia. 

If 3 genes deleted then there is a severe anaemia. Beta chains form tetramers (HbH) that damage RBCs. HbH is seen on electrophoresis. Fetus is ok but starts struggling when born

If 4 genes are deleted then it is letal in utero (hydrops fetalis). Gamma chain form tetramers (Hb Barts) that damage RBCs and is seen on electrophoresis. 

### Beta Thalassaemia 

Due to gene mutations (as compared to gene deletion)

Two beta genes that are present on chromosome 11. Mutations result in absent (beta null) or diminished production of beta (beta plus) globin chain. 

One normal beta and one beta plus - Beta thalassaemia minor usually asymptomatic with increased RBC count. 
- Findings
	- Microcytic hypochromic RBCs and target cells on smear. 
	- Target cell is when haemoglobin builds up in the centre of the cell this creates a red dot in the central pallor of RBCs. 
	- To see a target cell, you have to reduce the haemoglobin amount in the red blood cell, creating excess membrane. The only key finding is that there is an isolated increase in Hb A2.

2 beta nulls - Beta thalassaemia major which is the most severe form of the disease.  
- Presentation
	- Presents with severe anaemia a few months after birth because fetal haemoglobin is temporarily protective and doesn't have any beta chains
	- Alpha tetramers aggregate and damage the RBC. this leads to ineffective erythropoiesis and extravascular haemolysis (becuase the spleen detects weird RBCs and destroys it).
	- Because of the severe anaemia patients get massive erythroid hyperplasia. Expansion of haematopoeisis into marrow of skull and facial bones. Extramedullary haematopoiesis with hepatosplenomegaly. 
	-  Risk of aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19.  Parvovirus infects erythroid precursors and shuts them down, in most patients it is self limiting because of the reserve of precursors. However, in beta thalassaemia this is very dangerous. 
- Management
	- Chronic transfusions are often necessary which leads to risk for secondary haemochromatosis.
- Findings
	- On blood smear there is a microcytic hypochromic target cells and nucleated red blood cells (becuase red blood cells are being made in an abnormal location). 
	-   Electrophoresis - no HbA and there is increased HBA2 and HbF.
